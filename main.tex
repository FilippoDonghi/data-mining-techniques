\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

This report describes our solution for Assignment 2 of Data Mining Techniques. The task is to rank hotel properties per search query (\texttt{srch\_id}) so that booked hotels appear at the top, followed by clicked hotels, measured with NDCG@5 on the Kaggle competition server.

The data contains approximately five million rows for training and five million for testing. Each row is one displayed property for one search query, with rich contextual, property, and competitor information. We followed the data mining workflow suggested by the assignment, moving from related work and business framing into exploratory analysis, then feature engineering and the split strategy, then modeling and evaluation with at least two methods, and finally deployment considerations including ethical AI bias analysis and mitigation.

Our main model is a LightGBM LambdaMART ranker. It improves strongly over a recommender-style popularity baseline, and a reweighted variant reduces the performance gap between family and non-family queries.

\section{Related Work and Business Understanding}
\label{sec:related}

The business objective is to improve ranking quality in hotel search, because better ranking increases user satisfaction and conversion (clicks/bookings). This is a recommender systems problem with context-aware ranking constraints.

Prior recommender literature highlights three relevant ideas. First, collaborative and latent-factor methods are effective at capturing user-item preference patterns \cite{koren2009matrix}. Second, pairwise and listwise ranking methods (e.g., LambdaMART) are highly suitable when the evaluation target is NDCG \cite{burges2010ranknet}. Third, implicit-feedback recommenders (click/view/book interactions) require careful weighting of signal strength \cite{rendle2009bpr}.

Work from the original Expedia personalized hotel search challenge also emphasizes the value of hotel characteristics, location attractiveness, user purchase history, and competitor information, and reports combining multiple ranking models for the final hotel ordering \cite{liu2013expedia}. This motivated our two-model setup:
\begin{itemize}
\item A recommender-style popularity model with Bayesian smoothing (strong interpretable baseline).
\item A feature-rich LightGBM LambdaMART model as our final method.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exploratory Findings}
We performed EDA on missingness, target sparsity, and key relationships:
\begin{itemize}
\item Competitor fields (\texttt{comp\*}) and historical visitor fields have substantial missingness, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:missing}.
\item \textbf{Position bias:} when results use Expedia's own sort (\texttt{random\_bool=0}), hotels near the top are booked far more often simply because of where they sit on the page. Under random sort (\texttt{random\_bool=1}) the booking rate stays almost flat across positions (Figure~\ref{fig:posbias}). This finding is what led us to compute the popularity priors from random-sort rows only.
\item Price has a non-linear relationship with relevance. Very low and very high prices can both underperform depending on context, and raw price spans seven orders of magnitude because of mixed country conventions.
\item Relative features inside a search list (e.g., price rank within \texttt{srch\_id}) are more informative than absolute values alone.
\end{itemize}

\section{Data Preparation}
\label{sec:prep}

\subsection{Target Construction}
Following the competition definition, we used graded relevance:
\[
\text{relevance} = 5 \cdot \texttt{booking\_bool} + 1 \cdot (\texttt{click\_bool} \land \neg \texttt{booking\_bool}).
\]
This keeps booked items as the highest-signal outcomes while still rewarding clicks.

\subsection{Train/Validation Strategy}
To avoid direct query overlap, we split by \texttt{srch\_id} (query-level split), not by row. This prevents rows from the same search appearing in both train and validation. The split is random rather than temporal, however, and does not address future-data drift. The historical implementation also builds target encodings on a training fold and applies them back to its training rows; a stronger follow-up should use out-of-fold encodings.

\subsection{Feature Engineering}
We engineered six groups of features:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Raw context/property features:} site, destination, stars, review score, promotion, booking window, party size, etc.
\item \textbf{Price cleaning:} \texttt{price\_usd} is clipped at \$10{,}000 and log-transformed, and the per-night price (\texttt{price\_usd} / \texttt{srch\_length\_of\_stay}) is transformed the same way. Raw prices span seven orders of magnitude because of mixed country conventions such as per-night versus whole-stay pricing and prices shown with or without tax, which makes log-scaling necessary.
\item \textbf{Competitor signals:} counts of competitors where Expedia is cheaper, pricier, or unavailable, plus the maximum absolute price difference. These replace na\"ive column means over the mostly-missing competitor fields.
\item \textbf{Within-query relative features:} z-scores of log-price, location score, star rating, review score, and log-historical price within each \texttt{srch\_id}. We also add rank percentiles of price, location score, star rating, and review score, along with the query length, meaning the number of candidate hotels per search.
\item \textbf{Popularity priors} (fit on the training fold only): Bayesian-smoothed property click-through and booking rates, mean historical position, destination-property relevance, destination-star affinity, and overall destination booking rate.
\item \textbf{Visitor-history and temporal features:} differences from historical star and price preferences, total party size, per-person per-night price, month, and day-of-week.
\end{itemize}

For missing values, we relied on LightGBM's native NaN handling for raw features and used smoothed fallback means for target-encoded priors.

\section{Modeling and Evaluation}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Metric}
We evaluated with NDCG@5 per query and averaged over queries, matching the competition objective.

\subsection{Technique 1: Recommender-Style Popularity Baseline}
As a recommender systems technique, we ranked hotels using historical popularity priors with Bayesian smoothing:
\begin{itemize}
\item Global property signal: smoothed click-through rate per \texttt{prop\_id} (\texttt{prop\_ctr}).
\item Destination-conditional signal: smoothed relevance for destination-property pairs.
\item Final score: equal-weighted average of the two priors, 0.5 each.
\end{itemize}

This baseline is simple, scalable, and interpretable, and it captures collaborative signal from aggregate user interactions.

\subsection{Technique 2: LightGBM LambdaMART}
Our main model is \texttt{LGBMRanker} with the LambdaMART objective, a listwise learning-to-rank algorithm that directly optimises NDCG \cite{burges2010ranknet}. The model receives a \emph{group} parameter specifying the number of rows per query, enabling it to compare candidates within the same search. Key parameters:
\begin{itemize}
\item \texttt{learning\_rate=0.05}, \texttt{num\_leaves=255}
\item \texttt{feature\_fraction=0.85}, \texttt{bagging\_fraction=0.85}, \texttt{bagging\_freq=5}
\item \texttt{min\_child\_samples=100}
\item \texttt{label\_gain=[0,1,0,0,0,31]} (maps relevance 0/1/5 to gains $2^r-1$)
\item Early stopping on validation NDCG@5 with patience 100, with the best iteration found at 284
\end{itemize}

\subsection{What Did Not Work Well}
Four observations from our iteration cycle:
\begin{itemize}
\item A pointwise regression objective (MSE on graded labels) ranked poorly compared to LambdaMART because it does not directly optimise the list-level NDCG criterion.
\item Within-query relative features such as z-scores and rank percentiles sit near the top of the gain-importance plot. Earlier iterations that leaned more on raw absolute features ranked worse at the top, which matches the recommender literature on the value of relative signals within a list.
\item Plain column-wise means over the mostly-missing competitor fields (94--98\% missing) would mostly average noise, so we encoded them as counts of competitors where Expedia is cheaper, pricier, or sold out. The count form is naturally immune to the missing values, because a missing comparison simply does not add to any count.
\item Feeding hotel and destination identifiers to LightGBM as native categorical features lowered validation NDCG@5. Their high cardinality let the trees memorise individual hotels and overfit the training fold, so the identifiers were kept only as inputs to target encoding.
\end{itemize}

\section{Deployment and Ethical AI}
\label{sec:deploy}

\subsection{Scalable Deployment Discussion}
A production deployment can be implemented as a two-stage architecture:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Offline batch layer:} refresh popularity priors and retrain LambdaMART model daily/weekly.
\item \textbf{Online ranking layer:} compute lightweight query-relative features in real time, score candidate hotels, and return top-$k$ list.
\item \textbf{Monitoring:} track NDCG proxies, CTR/booking rates, latency, drift, and fairness gaps over time.
\end{itemize}

The feature design in this report fits this architecture well, since heavy aggregates are precomputed offline while per-query transforms stay low-latency.

\subsection{Bias Detection}
We audited group fairness across two user-query segments:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Family queries:} \texttt{srch\_children\_count > 0}
\item \textbf{Non-family queries:} \texttt{srch\_children\_count = 0}
\end{itemize}

Using NDCG@5 by group as the fairness metric:
\begin{itemize}
\item Before mitigation: family 0.4063, non-family 0.3841, gap 0.0222.
\end{itemize}

Family queries score higher on this split. Query specificity and label distribution are possible explanations, but the experiment does not establish a cause. The observed gap is an exploratory performance diagnostic rather than proof of unequal treatment.

\subsection{Bias Mitigation and Effectiveness}
We applied an exploratory \textbf{pre-processing reweighting} technique. During model training, rows from family queries received weight 1.6 while other rows kept weight 1.0. The value was a coursework choice and was not selected on a separate tuning split.

After mitigation:
\begin{itemize}
\item Family NDCG@5: 0.4040
\item Non-family NDCG@5: 0.3847
\item Gap: 0.0193
\end{itemize}

The weighted run raised non-family NDCG@5 from 0.3841 to 0.3847 while family NDCG@5 decreased from 0.4063 to 0.4040. Together this narrowed the observed gap from 0.0222 to 0.0193, about 13 percent, with a small global NDCG@5 decrease from 0.3895 to 0.3894. We did not estimate confidence intervals, test the weight on an independent holdout, or establish that family status is a protected or causal factor. The result should therefore be treated as an exploratory subgroup experiment, not evidence that fairness was achieved.

\section{What We Learned}
\label{sec:learned}

This assignment improved our understanding in three ways:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Ranking mindset:} optimizing query-level top-$k$ quality is different from standard classification.
\item \textbf{Feature engineering impact:} relative within-query signals were more valuable than many raw features.
\item \textbf{Responsible modeling:} fairness auditing and mitigation can be integrated with small performance cost.
\end{itemize}

Main challenges were data scale, missingness, and balancing relevance optimization with fairness. Unexpectedly, a simple popularity recommender already gave a strong baseline, showing the value of historical interaction priors in travel recommendation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We developed and evaluated two ranking approaches for hotel recommendation, a recommender-style popularity baseline at a local validation NDCG@5 of 0.2417 and a LightGBM LambdaMART model with engineered contextual features at a local validation NDCG@5 of 0.3895. The historical result combines a dedicated ranking objective, price cleaning, target-encoded popularity priors, within-query relative features, and destination-level collaborative signals. Reproducing it requires the restricted course data, and stronger follow-up work should add out-of-fold target encoding, temporal validation, and statistically grounded subgroup evaluation before making production or fairness claims.
